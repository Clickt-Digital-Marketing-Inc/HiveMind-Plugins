# The Adversarial Battery

Tests 1-8 run **with the teammate, out loud**, before writing. Not as a checklist you complete silently and summarize — as a conversation they participate in. Test 9 runs on yourself, at write time.

A director will run most of these in the first thirty seconds. The only question is whether the teammate hears them from you first, in private, where the answer can still change the proposal.

## How to run it

- **Scale to stakes.** Light ideas: tests 1 and 2. Standard: 1-5. Heavy: 1-8.
- **Test 9 is not scaled.** It runs whenever the memo contains a number you computed, at any stakes. It is the only test aimed at the drafter rather than the teammate — which makes it the only one nobody else will catch you skipping.
- **Attack the idea, not the teammate.** "This is what a director will hit you with" keeps you on the same side of the table. You are their sparring partner, not their examiner.
- **Push once past the first confident answer.** The first answer is usually rehearsed. The second is usually true.
- **Record every finding as Fixed, Accepted, or Open.** Never silently drop one because they sounded sure.

---

## 1. The kill shot

**Ask:** "What single fact, if it turned out to be true, would end this idea? Do you know whether it's true?"

This is the highest-leverage question in the battery. Every proposal has one. Most teammates know it and have been avoiding it.

> **Worked example — automated client reports**
>
> Kill shot: clients actually value the AM's manual read of their account, and an auto-drafted report reads generic enough to damage renewal conversations.
>
> "Do you know whether clients read the narrative or skip to the numbers?"
> "...no."
>
> → **Open.** And it's load-bearing enough that the memo's ask may need to shrink to finding out.

If the teammate can't name a kill shot, that's a finding in itself — they haven't tried to break it yet, and you're doing it for the first time together.

## 2. The do-nothing baseline

**Ask:** "If we just don't do this, what actually happens over the next six months?"

Proposals are written against an imagined disaster. Often the honest answer is "things continue, slightly annoyingly." A director will find that out. Better it's in the memo.

> "If we never build this, what happens?"
> "AMs keep spending the time."
> "And what does that cost us — do we lose accounts, miss deadlines, burn people out?"
> "...it's mostly that it's tedious."
>
> → The memo cannot claim a retention or capacity crisis. The honest case is quality of work life and reclaimed hours, which is a real case — just a different one, and one that survives contact.

Doing nothing must appear as an option in the memo whenever it's viable.

## 3. The cheaper alternative

**Ask:** "What's the version of this that takes 10% of the effort and gets most of the benefit? Why isn't that the proposal?"

Directors reflexively look for the cheap version. If one exists and the memo doesn't address it, the memo loses.

> "If most of those hours are pulling numbers rather than writing prose, a shared data pull into a doc gets most of the win without a build. What's the split?"
> "Honestly, probably mostly gathering."
>
> → **Fixed.** The proposal changes: measure the split first, then decide between the cheap fix and the build.

This test changes proposals more than any other. Take it seriously even when it's unwelcome.

## 4. The director's first objection

**Ask:** "Picture the most skeptical person in that room. What do they say in the first thirty seconds?"

The teammate almost always knows. Getting it named means the memo can answer it in advance, which is the difference between a memo that survives the room and one that gets sent back.

> "Sarah's going to say we tried automating reports two years ago and it went nowhere."
> "Did we? What happened?"
> "...I think it stalled because nobody owned it."
>
> → **Fixed.** The memo names the prior attempt, why it failed, and what's different now. Pre-empting a known objection is worth more than any benefit bullet.

## 5. The second-order cost

**Ask:** "It's built and launched. Who maintains it? What breaks when a platform changes its API? What does year two cost?"

Proposals are costed at the build and free forever after. Directors have been burned by this and will look for it.

> "Who owns it when it breaks mid-month-end?"
> "I guess whoever built it."
> "Is that a named person with time allocated, or a hope?"
>
> → **Accepted or Open.** Either name an owner or record that ongoing ownership is unresolved. Do not let it vanish.

## 6. The incentive check

**Ask:** "Who has to change how they work for this to succeed, and what's in it for them?"

Plans that require behaviour change from people with no reason to change are the most common quiet failure. This test catches it.

> "AMs need to review and edit the drafts rather than rubber-stamp them."
> "What happens if they rubber-stamp?"
> "A weak report goes to a client with our name on it."
> "So the thing that makes this safe is exactly the step under time pressure at month-end?"
>
> → **Accepted**, explicitly, in the memo. A director should decide knowing that.

## 7. The reversal

**Ask:** "Suppose this already existed and someone proposed removing it. Would that sound reasonable?"

A clean way to test whether the value is real or just novelty. If removing it sounds fine, the case is thinner than it looks.

> "If we already had auto-drafted reports and someone said let's go back to writing them by hand, would anyone defend that?"
> "God, no. AMs would riot."
>
> → Genuine value. Say so in the memo — it's a stronger argument than any projection.

## 8. The evidence audit

**Ask:** "Which of the numbers in this case did someone actually count?"

Walk every load-bearing claim the teammate gave you and force it into measured, estimated, or assumed. Assumptions wearing numbers are the thing that gets a memo dismantled in a room.

Note what this test cannot see: it asks who *counted* a number, and nobody counted the ones you computed. Anything you derived passes this test trivially and is handled by test 9 — which is why test 9 exists and why skipping it leaves the largest hole in the battery.

> - "40 reports a month" → **Measured.** Client roster.
> - "3-5 hours each" → **Estimated.** One AM's recollection across six accounts.
> - "AI cuts it by half" → **Assumed.** No pilot. Load-bearing for the whole case.
>
> → The third one is the memo. Everything rests on it and nobody has tested it. That reframes the ask from "approve a build" to "approve a two-week test", which is both more honest and far more likely to get a yes.

## 9. The model audit — run this one on yourself

**Ask yourself, not the teammate:** "Which numbers in this memo did I compute rather than receive? For each one — what am I comparing against, and did I choose that baseline or did they?"

Tests 1-8 police what the teammate told you. This one polices what you built on top of it. Every input can be honest and the conclusion still wrong, because **the error lives in the model, not the arithmetic.**

Three failure modes, in the order they bite:

- **The zero baseline.** You compared your option's gross value against nothing. The real comparison is the next-best use of the same money — usually already sitting in your own Options section. Compute the *marginal* gain over that alternative, not the absolute gain over a void.
- **The line item counted twice.** A cost that is "already committed, so it doesn't count against us" in one section cannot be "what we're risking" in another, or "what we'd reallocate" in a third. Read your own draft for every figure appearing in two places and check it is doing the same job in both.
- **The asymmetry that isn't.** "Small downside, huge upside" is the most seductive sentence in memo-writing and the easiest to get backwards. If the downside excludes the value forgone by *not* taking the alternative, it is not the downside.

> **Worked example — a real failure, from this skill's own testing**
>
> A memo recommended a $9,150 spend with: *"downside ~$6,000, upside ~$100,000/year — cheap and asymmetric."*
>
> Every figure traced to the teammate. Every calculation was correct. Both numbers were still wrong:
>
> - **Upside** was measured against zero — while the memo's *own Option C*, three lines above, said the alternative returned $64,615. Real marginal upside: **~$44,000**, inflated 2.3×.
> - **Downside** counted only the admittance — ignoring the $58,615 net that Option C would have returned. Real exposure: **~$68,000**, understated ~11×.
> - And the admittance was declared "committed, spent regardless" in one section while being reallocated in another. It cannot be both.
>
> The recommendation may still have been right. Every number supporting it was not, and two were wrong in the flattering direction. **This passed a ten-point validation checklist clean.**

If test 9 finds nothing, check you actually ran it rather than agreed with yourself.

## After the battery

Every finding lands in the memo:

- **Fixed** → the plan changed. What Would Kill This says what changed and why.
- **Accepted** → a real risk, consciously taken. What Would Kill This says so, without a consoling mitigation stapled on.
- **Open** → nobody knows. Open Questions, with who could find out.

If the battery produced nothing, you ran it too gently. Every real proposal has at least one honest weakness. Finding zero means the teammate was defending rather than thinking — go back to test 1 and push past the rehearsed answer.
